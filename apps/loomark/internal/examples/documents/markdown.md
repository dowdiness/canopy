# A Writer's Guide to Markdown

Markdown is a way of marking up plain text with a few ordinary characters. You
write in Text, and Preview sets the same words as a page. Nothing is hidden:
every mark you see in Text is all there is.

Open Split to watch each example below take shape as you read it.

---

## Headings

Begin a line with `#` and a space to make a title. Add more marks for smaller
headings, down to six levels.

### A section

#### A smaller section

##### A note within it

###### The quietest heading

## Emphasis

Surround words with one asterisk for *italic*, two for **bold**, and three for
***both at once***. Emphasis can nest, *like a thought with **one word**
pressed harder*. Use it sparingly, the way you would raise your voice.

## Paragraphs and line breaks

A blank line starts a new paragraph. A single line break inside a paragraph is
treated as a space, so you can wrap lines in Text however you like.

When a line must end exactly where you choose, finish it with a backslash \
and the next words begin on a new line, as in a poem or an address.

## Lists

### When the order does not matter

- A notebook that opens flat
- Something warm to drink
- An hour with no one asking for it
  - Early morning
  - Or late at night
    - After the house is asleep
- A window

### When it does

1. Write the draft from beginning to end
2. Put it away for a day
3. Read it aloud
   1. Mark every sentence that made you stumble
   2. Mark every sentence that made you smile
4. Revise the first kind, keep the second

### Things still to do

- [ ] Find a better word for "suddenly"
- [ ] Decide whether the letter is ever sent

## Quotations

Begin a line with `>` to quote someone, or to set a passage apart.

> Writing is a way of finding out what you think. The page holds still long
> enough for you to look at the thought.
>
> > A quotation can hold another, the way a letter can hold a pressed flower.

## A small reference card

Inline marks like `**bold**` stay visible in Text. A fenced block keeps every
character exactly as typed, which makes it a good place to keep the marks
themselves:

```markdown
# Title
## Section

*italic*  **bold**  `code`

- a list item
1. a numbered item
- [ ] a task

> a quotation

[a link](https://example.com)
![a picture's description](picture.jpg)

---
```

## A page to keep reading

The first draft of anything is a conversation with yourself. You do not yet
know what you mean, so you write in order to find out. It is allowed to be
clumsy. It is allowed to repeat itself, to wander into a side room and stay
there too long, to end in the middle of a sentence because the kettle boiled.

What matters is that the words exist. A blank page can only be imagined; a bad
page can be improved. Somewhere in the third or fourth paragraph, a sentence
will arrive that sounds more like you than anything before it. Underline it.
Begin the next draft there.

Revision is a different kind of attention. You read slowly, as a stranger
would, and notice where the stranger's eyes would drift. You take out the
words that were scaffolding. You move the ending to the beginning, or discover
that the beginning was never needed at all.

It helps to change where you read. A draft read as a finished page instead
of in an editor makes familiar sentences unfamiliar enough to see clearly. That is one reason Preview exists: to let
you meet your own writing as a reader would.

And then, at some point, you stop. Not because the piece is perfect, but
because it has become itself. You can tell the difference the way you can
tell when bread is done: it sounds hollow when you tap it, and the kitchen
smells of something finished.

Close the notebook. Tomorrow there will be another page.
